\chapter{Controller, Actuation and Simulation \textbf{David Jung}}
\label{ch:controller-and-simulation}

The primary requirement for the satellite is to fully deploy and retract the umbrella mechanism on demand. To fulfill this operational objective, a motorized system is mechanically linked with the umbrella assembly, enabling robust bidirectional movement control throughout the deployment and stowage phases.

This chapter presents the design, implementation, and verification of the controller and simulation environment for the motorized umbrella mechanism. The contents of this chapter are structured as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Requirements:} Defines the system and simulation environment requirements for the motorized umbrella mechanism.
    \item \textbf{Motor and Transmission Selection:} Details the selection criteria and trade-offs for the electric motor and transmission mechanism (e.g., gear ratio, torque capacity, movement,efficiency) to fulfill the deployment requirements.
    \item \textbf{Controller Architecture \& Strategy:} Details the control loops and actuation algorithms designed to regulate speed, position, and torque during deployment and retraction.
    \item \textbf{Simulation Setup \& Dynamic Modeling:} Describes the numerical dynamic simulation model created to evaluate control performance under space operational conditions.
    \item \textbf{Results \& Performance Evaluation:} Analyzes simulation outcomes, focusing on deployment accuracy, energy consumption, and structural stability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Selection of Motor and Transmission}
\label{sec:selection-of-motor-and-transmission}

This section outlines the selection process for the electric motor and transmission mechanism required to actuate the deployable umbrella system. The selection criteria are derived from the system requirements, taking into account required torque, speed regulation, mechanical locking capability, and operational constraints in space environments.

\subsection{Selection of the Motor}
\label{subsec:selection-of-the-motor}

The electric motor selection is governed by four key system requirements:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bidirectional Torque (Req.~1.1):} Requires a motor drive capable of four-quadrant operation to supply positive torque during deployment and negative torque during controlled retraction.
    \item \textbf{Thermal Range (Req.~8):} Dictates motor windings, housing materials, and bearings rated for temperature extremes between $-60\,^\circ\text{C}$ and $+80\,^\circ\text{C}$.
    \item \textbf{Atmospheric \& Vacuum Compatibility (Req.~9, Req.~10):} Operating in a vacuum eliminates convective heat dissipation, requiring dedicated conductive mounting and thermal paths to dissipate motor heat.

\end{itemize}

The primary challenge in motor selection and operation stems from the upper limit of the thermal operating range. Standard DC or BLDC motors utilize permanent magnets that begin to soften and undergo irreversible demagnetization around $100\,^\circ\text{C}$. Given a maximum ambient environment temperature of $+80\,^\circ\text{C}$ combined with internal thermal losses during operation, this breakdown threshold can easily be exceeded. Consequently, the motor selection necessitates high-temperature magnet materials (such as Samarium-Cobalt or high-coercivity Neodymium grades) paired with effective thermal management.

To address these thermal and environmental constraints, motor solutions from Maxon's Heavy Duty (HD) series represent a suitable choice\footnote{Maxon Heavy Duty Motors Technical Specification: \url{https://maxonjapan.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Brosch_OilGas_2020.pdf}}. Motors in this series feature Samarium-Cobalt (SmCo) magnets capable of operating across a thermal range of $-55\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $+200\,^\circ\text{C}$, safely remaining within operational limits even under severe ambient and resistive heat loads. Furthermore, this series is engineered to withstand mechanical shocks up to $1000\,g$ and continuous vibration levels of $25\,g_{\text{rms}}$, ensuring full structural integrity and operational capability after enduring the high vibration loads associated with rocket launch.

As the exact electrical specifications of the underlying FlatSat system have not yet been provided by the Supmeca project team, a final hardware motor selection cannot be finalized at this stage. For dynamic modeling, control design, and performance evaluation, the Hochschule Esslingen project team selected the \textit{Maxon EC-4pole 30} (24\,V Space/Aerospace configuration). \subsection{Selection of the Transmission}
\label{subsec:selection-of-the-transmission}

The selection of the transmission system is primarily driven by Requirement~3 and Requirement~3.1 as the major requirements, ensuring that the deployable umbrella mechanism maintains a stable final position and remains mechanically locked without requiring continuous energy consumption.

Furthermore, the exact hardware gear ratio cannot be finalized at this stage due to missing technical specifications for the FlatSat system and the unfinalized final motor selection. However, for the dynamic simulation setup with the selected reference motor, a gear ratio of $i = 20$ is currently utilized.

To provide the required locking functionality without having to supply continuous torque from the motor, a self-locking transmission is necessary. For this specific use case, a self-locking worm gear mechanism was chosen.

The benefit of a self-locking worm gear is that whenever the input (motor side) stops providing torque, the transmission locks the output into place and provides the necessary holding torque to keep the structure in a rigid fixed position. A self-locking worm gear (or irreversible worm gear) is a gear setup where power can transfer from the worm (screw) to the worm gear (wheel), but not in reverse. If torque is applied directly to the worm wheel, friction at the thread interface prevents the worm from rotating, acting as a natural mechanical brake.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Key Variables and Definitions for Self-Locking Worm Gear}
    \label{tab:worm-gear-variables}
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|c|Z|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Definition} \\
        \hline
        $\gamma$ & Lead angle of the worm \\
        \hline
        $p_L$ & Lead of the worm thread ($p_L = \pi \cdot d_1 \cdot \tan\gamma$) \\
        \hline
        $d_1$ & Pitch diameter of the worm \\
        \hline
        $z_1$ & Number of threads (starts) on the worm \\
        \hline
        $m_n$ & Normal module \\
        \hline
        $\mu_s$ & Static coefficient of friction between worm and gear teeth \\
        \hline
        $\phi_n$ & Normal pressure angle (typically $14.5^\circ$, $20^\circ$, or $25^\circ$) \\
        \hline
        $\rho'$ & Friction angle adjusted for tooth pressure angle \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Governing Equations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lead Angle Formula:}
    The lead angle $\gamma$ depends on the pitch diameter of the worm $d_1$, the axial pitch, and the number of starts $z_1$:
    \begin{equation}
        \tan \gamma = \frac{z_1 \cdot m_n}{d_1} = \frac{p_L}{\pi \cdot d_1}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Effective Friction Angle ($\rho'$):}
    Because the gear teeth are inclined at normal pressure angle $\phi_n$, the normal force increases, raising the effective friction coefficient $\mu'$:
    \begin{equation}
        \mu' = \frac{\mu_s}{\cos \phi_n}
    \end{equation}
    The effective friction angle $\rho'$ is given by:
    \begin{equation}
        \rho' = \arctan(\mu') = \arctan\left(\frac{\mu_s}{\cos \phi_n}\right)
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{The Self-Locking Condition:}
    For a worm gear to be statically self-locking, the lead angle must be strictly smaller than the effective friction angle:
    \begin{equation}
        \gamma < \rho'
    \end{equation}
    Alternatively, expressed in terms of the static friction coefficient:
    \begin{equation}
        \tan \gamma < \frac{\mu_s}{\cos \phi_n}
    \end{equation}
    \textit{Rule of Thumb:} In engineering practice, self-locking generally occurs when the lead angle $\gamma$ is less than $5^\circ$ (or $3^\circ$--$4^\circ$ for enhanced safety under vibrating operational conditions).

    \item \textbf{Gear Efficiency Equations:}
    Efficiency is directly linked to self-locking capacity. Low lead angles create high friction, which reduces forward efficiency.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Forward Efficiency (Worm Drives Wheel):} When driving normally from the worm shaft:
        \begin{equation}
            \eta = \frac{\tan \gamma}{\tan(\gamma + \rho')}
        \end{equation}
        \item \textbf{Reverse Efficiency (Wheel Drives Worm):} When attempting to back-drive from the wheel:
        \begin{equation}
            \eta_{\text{rev}} = \frac{\tan(\gamma - \rho')}{\tan \gamma}
        \end{equation}
    \end{itemize}
    Notice that if $\gamma \le \rho'$, then $\tan(\gamma - \rho') \le 0$, resulting in a reverse efficiency $\eta_{\text{rev}} \le 0$. This mathematical zero or negative efficiency represents the self-locking threshold.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Controller}
\label{sec:controller}

To fulfill the control and operational requirements of the motorized umbrella mechanism---specifically ensuring full controllability of position and speed during deployment and retraction (Req.~2 in Table~\ref{tab:system-requirements}), adhering to the required actuation time limits (Req.~4 and Req.~5 in Table~\ref{tab:system-requirements}), and integrating closed-loop control across speed, position, and torque (Req.~2 in Table~\ref{tab:simulation-requirements})---a cascaded position and speed controller architecture was chosen.


Figure~\ref{fig:cascaded-controller} illustrates the cascaded PID control structure employed for position and velocity regulation. In this hierarchical control scheme, the outer position controller processes the target position error to generate a target velocity command for the inner velocity loop. The inner speed controller then regulates the velocity and outputs a corresponding current or torque command to drive the motor. The key benefit of this structure in the scoope of this project is the breakdown of a higher order system into two lower order systems which can be tuned independently.
    \begin{figure}[htbp]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/controller/cascaded_position_speed_pid.jpg}
        \caption{Cascaded position and speed control structure with inner and outer loops~\cite{doc:synapticon}.}
        \label{fig:cascaded-controller}
    \end{figure}

\paragraph{Outer Position Control Loop (P Controller)}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}(t) = \left(\text{Pos}_{\text{ref}}(t) - \text{Pos}_{\text{meas}}(t)\right) \cdot \text{KP}_{\text{pos}}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Inner Speed Control Loop (PI Controller)}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Torque}_{\text{cmd}}(t) = \left(\text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}(t) - \text{Vel}_{\text{meas}}(t)\right) \cdot \text{KP}_{\text{vel}} + \int_{0}^{t} \left(\text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}(t) - \text{Vel}_{\text{meas}}(t)\right) \cdot \text{KI}_{\text{vel}} \, dt
\end{equation}

\newpage
\subsection{Realization of a Cascaded Controller}
\label{subsec:realization-of-a-cascaded-controller}

To realize this cascaded controller in real life, a discrete integration method is needed. To keep it simple and fast, an explicit Euler integration approach is used for the inner velocity loop. In addition the integral part is clamped/limited to account for actuator limits and prevent wind up. The following pseudo code shows the realization of the controller.


\begin{lstlisting}[style=vscodelight, caption={Discrete realization of the cascaded position (P) and speed (PI) controller using explicit Euler integration.}, label={lst:cascaded-pi-euler}]
// PI Update (Explicit Euler: integral += Ki * error * dt)
function update_pi(error, dt, Kp, Ki, ref_integral, min_val, max_val):
    ref_integral = clamp(ref_integral + Ki * error * dt, min_val, max_val)
    return clamp(Kp * error + ref_integral, min_val, max_val)

// Cascaded Step
function control_step(pos_target, pos_actual, speed_actual, dt):
    // Outer Loop: Position -> Speed setpoint (P Controller)
    speed_target = clamp(Kp_pos * (pos_target - pos_actual), -MAX_SPEED, MAX_SPEED)
    
    // Inner Loop: Speed -> Torque demand (PI Controller)
    torque_out   = update_pi(speed_target - speed_actual, dt, Kp_vel, Ki_vel, spd_int, -MAX_TORQUE, MAX_TORQUE)
    
    return torque_out
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Modeling}
\label{sec:modeling}

The modeling was performed through a combination of Simulink and Simscape. This framework was chosen to enable rapid prototyping on the controller side while maintaining direct coupling with the multibody mechanics of the system.

\subsection{Modeling of the Controller}
\label{subsec:simulation-of-the-controller}

Figure~\ref{fig:pi-speed-controller} shows the Simulink implementation of the inner PI speed controller operating with integral anti-windup clamping.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/controller/PI_Speed_Controller.png}
    \caption{Simulink implementation of the PI speed controller featuring integral anti-windup clamping.}
    \label{fig:pi-speed-controller}
\end{figure}

The block diagram directly realizes the discrete speed controller algorithm introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:realization-of-a-cascaded-controller} and Listing~\ref{lst:cascaded-pi-euler}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Proportional Path:} The velocity error $e_{\text{vel}} = \text{Vel}_{\text{ref}} - \text{Vel}_{\text{meas}}$ is multiplied by the velocity proportional gain $\text{KP}_{\text{vel}}$ (\texttt{KP\_speed}) to generate the immediate proportional control response.
    \item \textbf{Discrete Euler Integration:} In the lower integral path, the velocity error $e_{\text{vel}}$ is scaled by the velocity integral gain $\text{KI}_{\text{vel}}$ (\texttt{KI\_speed}) and multiplied by the sample time $\Delta t$ (\texttt{STEP}). It is then fed into a discrete accumulator formed by a summation junction and a unit delay block $\frac{1}{z}$, implementing explicit Euler integration.
    \item \textbf{Integral Clamping (Anti-Windup):} Immediately following the delay block $\frac{1}{z}$, a saturation block limits the accumulated integral value within set upper and lower thresholds. By feeding the clamped state back into the summation loop, the accumulator state is capped to prevent integral windup during torque saturation.
    \item \textbf{Control Output Generation:} The proportional output and the clamped integral output are summed together to produce the final control signal $\text{Torque}_{\text{cmd}}$ (\texttt{controll\_value}), representing the torque command delivered to the actuation drive.
\end{itemize}

In addition to the speed controller, Figure~\ref{fig:p-position-controller} shows the Simulink implementation of the outer position loop, which incorporates a gain-switching position controller.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/controller/P_Position_Controller.png}
    \caption{Simulink implementation of the switching P-position controller with directional gain adaptation.}
    \label{fig:p-position-controller}
\end{figure}

The outer loop regulates the position error $e_{\text{pos}} = \text{Pos}_{\text{ref}} - \text{Pos}_{\text{meas}}$ (\texttt{angle\_delta}, where $\text{Pos}_{\text{ref}}$ corresponds to \texttt{desired\_position} and $\text{Pos}_{\text{meas}}$ to \texttt{shaft\_angle}) to command the reference velocity $\text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}$ for the inner speed controller. When the system is deployed and tested on Earth under gravitational influence, actuation dynamics become asymmetric: upward movement requires higher torque to overcome gravitational forces, whereas downward movement is assisted by gravity. 

To compensate for this gravitational bias and ensure uniform dynamic behavior, a dedicated MATLAB function block (\texttt{switching\_postion\_controll}) automatically selects the position proportional gain $\text{KP}_{\text{pos}}$ based on the sign of the position error $e_{\text{pos}}$:

\begin{equation}
    \text{KP}_{\text{pos}} = 
    \begin{cases} 
        \text{KP}_{\text{pos,up}} & \text{if } e_{\text{pos}} \ge 0 \quad (\text{upward actuation}), \\
        \text{KP}_{\text{pos,down}} & \text{if } e_{\text{pos}} < 0 \quad (\text{downward actuation}).
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The key functional aspects of the position controller implementation are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Position Error Calculation:} The feedback position $\text{Pos}_{\text{meas}}$ (\texttt{shaft\_angle}) is subtracted from reference position $\text{Pos}_{\text{ref}}$ (\texttt{desired\_position}) to yield the position error $e_{\text{pos}} = \text{Pos}_{\text{ref}} - \text{Pos}_{\text{meas}}$ (\texttt{angle\_delta}).
    \item \textbf{Directional Gain Switching:} The block \texttt{switching\_postion\_controll} evaluates the sign of $e_{\text{pos}}$ (\texttt{angle\_delta}). If $e_{\text{pos}} \ge 0$, it assigns $\text{KP}_{\text{pos,up}}$ (\texttt{KP\_pos\_up}); otherwise, it selects $\text{KP}_{\text{pos,down}}$ (\texttt{KP\_pos\_down}).
    \item \textbf{Velocity Command Output:} The selected position proportional gain $\text{KP}_{\text{pos}}$ scales the position error $e_{\text{pos}}$ to generate the target velocity reference signal $\text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}$, which is directly passed into the inner \texttt{speed\_controller} block.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Modeling of the Motor}
\label{subsec:simulation-of-the-motor}

Figure~\ref{fig:motor-model} shows the Simulink block diagram implementation of the DC motor model, capturing both the electro-dynamic transient response of the armature circuit and mechanical friction losses. The motor parameters utilized in the simulation model are listed in Table~\ref{tab:motor-parameters}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig/controller/motor_model.png}
    \caption{Simulink implementation of the DC motor dynamic model.}
    \label{fig:motor-model}
\end{figure}

The dynamic behavior of the motor simulation model is governed by the following electrical and mechanical equations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Armature Current Dynamic Equation:}
    Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law to the armature circuit yields the differential equation for armature current $i(t)$, accounting for resistive drop and back-electromotive force (back-EMF):
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{di(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{L_A} \left( V_{\text{input}}(t) - R_a \cdot i(t) - c_w \cdot \omega(t) \right)
    \end{equation}
    where $V_{\text{input}}(t)$ is the input voltage (\texttt{input\_voltage}), $R_a$ is the armature resistance, $L_A$ is the armature inductance, $c_w$ is the motor constant, and $\omega(t)$ is the output shaft velocity (\texttt{output\_shaft\_velocity}). In the Simulink block diagram, the net voltage sum is scaled by gain $1/L_A$ and integrated via $1/s$ to calculate the motor current $i(t)$ (\texttt{motor\_current}).

    \item \textbf{Electromagnetic Shaft Torque Equation:}
    The electromagnetic torque produced by the motor is proportional to the armature current $i(t)$ scaled by the motor constant $c_w$:
    \begin{equation}
        T_{\text{shaft}}(t) = c_w \cdot i(t)
    \end{equation}
    where $T_{\text{shaft}}(t)$ represents the internal electromagnetic shaft torque (\texttt{shaft\_torque}).

    \item \textbf{Net Output Motor Torque Equation:}
    The net motor output torque subtracts viscous damping losses from the generated shaft torque:
    \begin{equation}
        T_{\text{motor}}(t) = T_{\text{shaft}}(t) - C_{\text{Damp}} \cdot \omega(t) = c_w \cdot i(t) - C_{\text{Damp}} \cdot \omega(t)
    \end{equation}
    where $C_{\text{Damp}}$ is the viscous damping coefficient and $T_{\text{motor}}(t)$ is the net output torque (\texttt{motor\_torque}) delivered to the mechanical transmission.
\end{enumerate}

In the motor model itself, rotor inertia is not explicitly modeled within the block diagram. Due to the rigid mechanical connection to the transmission, the total inertia of the transmission input side, including the motor rotor, is modeled directly within the Simscape transmission model.

\label{subsec:simulation-of-the-transmission}

Figure~\ref{fig:transmission-model} shows the Simscape model implementation of the transmission. The transmission is modeled with a combination of Simscape's native worm gear block, inertia blocks, and damping blocks. In addition, a custom structure and logic are implemented to accurately represent a real-world self-locking transmission.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/controller/transmission_model.png}

Test
To organize the implementation architecture shown in Figure~\ref{fig:transmission-model}, the model components are categorized into three distinct groups:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Physical Components:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Worm Gear Block:} Represents the mechanical gear reduction ratio between the worm input shaft ($W$) and worm wheel output shaft ($G$).
        \item \textbf{Rotational Inertia Blocks:} Model the physical mass moment of inertia of the input worm shaft and output gear shaft.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Simulation Artifacts for Real-World Representation:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Controllable Friction Clutch \& Engagement Logic (\texttt{clutch\_controll}):} Implemented to dynamically engage and ensure the transmission locks fully in the real-world self-locking state.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Simulation Stability and Compatibility:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Rotational Damping Blocks:} Placed on the input and output shafts of the worm gear to provide essential numerical damping and prevent simulation crashes.
        \item \textbf{Simulink-to-Simscape Conversion:} Converts incoming Simulink torque signals (\texttt{motor\_shaft\_torque1}) into physical domain network torque using an Ideal Torque Source.
        \item \textbf{Simscape-to-Multibody Link Blocks:} Connects the transmission model directly to the multibody mechanics using interface ports (\texttt{mb\_torque} and \texttt{mb\_angular\_speed}) and motion sensors.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

To control the friction clutch (\texttt{clutch\_controll}) and accurately replicate self-locking behavior, a dedicated MATLAB function block evaluates the input torque using threshold-based hysteresis logic, as shown in Listing~\ref{lst:clutch-control}.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=vscodelight, language=Matlab, morekeywords={persistent, isempty}, caption={MATLAB function script governing clutch engagement logic for self-locking simulation.}, label={lst:clutch-control}]
function y = fcn(input_torque)
persistent y_state;

% Initialize y_state on the very first execution
if isempty(y_state)
    y_state = 0; 
end

if abs(input_torque) <= 10^-14
    y = 1;
    y_state = 1;
    return
elseif abs(input_torque) >= 10^-12
    y = 0;
    y_state = 0;
    return
end

y = y_state;
\end{lstlisting}

The function script operates according to the following principles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Persistent State Memory:} The persistent variable \texttt{y\_state} retains the engagement state across simulation time steps, preserving historical state information.
    \item \textbf{Locking Condition ($y=1$):} When the absolute input torque $|T_{\text{in}}| \le 10^{-14}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, the motor is effectively idle or unpowered. The output signal $y$ switches to $1$, fully engaging the clutch to lock the transmission.
    \item \textbf{Unlocking Condition ($y=0$):} When $|T_{\text{in}}| \ge 10^{-12}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, active driving torque is applied. The output signal $y$ transitions to $0$, disengaging the clutch to permit normal motion.
    \item \textbf{Hysteresis Band ($10^{-14} < |T_{\text{in}}| < 10^{-12}$):} Within the intermediate torque deadband, the clutch holds its previous state ($y = y_{\text{state}}$). This hysteresis prevents high-frequency chattering and numerical solver instability near zero torque.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Full Model}
\label{subsec:the-full-model}

The individual subsystems described in Sections~\ref{subsec:simulation-of-the-controller}, \ref{subsec:simulation-of-the-motor}, and~\ref{subsec:simulation-of-the-transmission} are integrated into a comprehensive closed-loop co-simulation model in Simulink and Simscape Multibody.

Figure~\ref{fig:full-model} displays the top-level Simulink architecture integrating the control system subsystem (\texttt{controll\_system}) with the physical satellite dynamics model (\texttt{sat\_mechanics}). The \texttt{controll\_system} block hosts both the cascaded position and speed controller as well as the DC motor model. It receives the target setpoint (\texttt{desired\_angle}), closed-loop feedback for measured position (\texttt{shaft\_angle}) and velocity (\texttt{shaft\_speed}), and an external clock signal (\texttt{clock\_input}) to drive discrete integration for velocity control. Based on these inputs, it outputs the generated actuation torque (\texttt{motor\_torque}), which drives the satellite mechanics block to update system states and close the loop.
