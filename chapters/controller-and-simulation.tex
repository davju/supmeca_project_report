\chapter{Controller, Actuation and Simulation}
\label{ch:controller-and-simulation}

The primary requirement for the satellite is to fully deploy and retract the umbrella mechanism on demand. To fulfill this operational objective, a motorized system is mechanically linked with the umbrella assembly, enabling robust bidirectional movement control throughout the deployment and stowage phases.

This chapter presents the design, implementation, and verification of the controller and simulation environment for the motorized umbrella mechanism. The contents of this chapter are structured as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simulation Requirements:} Defines the specific requirements for the dynamic simulation environment and closed-loop controller evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Motor and Transmission Selection:} Details the selection criteria and trade-offs for the electric motor and transmission mechanism (e.g., gear ratio, torque capacity, movement, efficiency) to fulfill the deployment requirements.
    \item \textbf{Controller Architecture \& Strategy:} Details the control loops and actuation algorithms designed to regulate speed, position, and torque during deployment and retraction.
    \item \textbf{Simulation Setup \& Dynamic Modeling:} Describes the numerical dynamic simulation model created to evaluate control performance under space operational conditions.
    \item \textbf{Results \& Performance Evaluation:} Analyzes simulation outcomes, focusing on deployment accuracy, energy consumption, and structural stability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Selection of Motor and Transmission}
\label{sec:selection-of-motor-and-transmission}

This section outlines the selection process for the electric motor and transmission mechanism required to actuate the deployable umbrella system. The selection criteria are derived from the system requirements defined in Table~\ref{tab:technical_requirements}, taking into account required torque, speed regulation, mechanical locking capability, and operational constraints in space environments.

\subsection{Selection of the Motor}
\label{subsec:selection-of-the-motor}

The electric motor selection is governed by key requirements established for the mechatronic and environmental subsystems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bidirectional Actuation and Torque Sizing (REQ-08, REQ-09):} Requires a motor drive capable of supplying sufficient torque for both deployment and controlled retraction under the estimated mechanism loads.
    \item \textbf{Thermal and Environmental Range (REQ-13):} Dictates motor windings, housing materials, and bearings rated for space thermal variations (e.g., $-60\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $+80\,^\circ\text{C}$).
    \item \textbf{Atmospheric \& Vacuum Compatibility (REQ-13, REQ-22):} Operating in a vacuum eliminates convective heat dissipation, requiring dedicated conductive mounting and thermal paths to dissipate motor heat during test-bench and space conditions.
\end{itemize}

The primary challenge in motor selection and operation stems from the upper limit of the thermal operating range. Standard DC or BLDC motors utilize permanent magnets that begin to soften and undergo irreversible demagnetization around $100\,^\circ\text{C}$. Given a maximum ambient environment temperature of $+80\,^\circ\text{C}$ combined with internal thermal losses during operation, this breakdown threshold can easily be exceeded. Consequently, the motor selection necessitates high-temperature magnet materials (such as Samarium-Cobalt or high-coercivity Neodymium grades) paired with effective thermal management.

To address these thermal and environmental constraints, motor solutions from Maxon's Heavy Duty (HD) series represent a suitable choice\footnote{Maxon Heavy Duty Motors Technical Specification: \url{https://maxonjapan.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Brosch_OilGas_2020.pdf}}. Motors in this series feature Samarium-Cobalt (SmCo) magnets capable of operating across a thermal range of $-55\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $+200\,^\circ\text{C}$, safely remaining within operational limits even under severe ambient and resistive heat loads. Furthermore, this series is engineered to withstand mechanical shocks up to $1000\,g$ and continuous vibration levels of $25\,g_{\text{rms}}$, ensuring full structural integrity and operational capability after enduring the high vibration loads associated with rocket launch.

As the exact electrical specifications of the underlying FlatSat system have not yet been provided by the Supmeca project team, a final hardware motor selection cannot be finalized at this stage. For dynamic modeling, control design, and performance evaluation, the Hochschule Esslingen project team selected the \textit{Maxon EC-4pole 30} (24\,V Space/Aerospace configuration). \subsection{Selection of the Transmission}
\label{subsec:selection-of-the-transmission}

The selection of the transmission system is primarily driven by REQ-06 and REQ-07 (Table~\ref{tab:technical_requirements}), ensuring that the deployable umbrella mechanism maintains a stable final position and incorporates a locking or self-locking mechanism to prevent unintended movement without requiring continuous energy consumption.

Furthermore, the exact hardware gear ratio cannot be finalized at this stage due to missing technical specifications for the FlatSat system and the unfinalized final motor selection. However, for the dynamic simulation setup with the selected reference motor, a gear ratio of $i = 20$ is currently utilized.

To provide the required locking functionality without having to supply continuous torque from the motor, a self-locking transmission is necessary. For this specific use case, a self-locking worm gear mechanism was chosen.

The benefit of a self-locking worm gear is that whenever the input (motor side) stops providing torque, the transmission locks the output into place and provides the necessary holding torque to keep the structure in a rigid fixed position. In a self-locking worm gear setup, torque can be transmitted from the worm (screw) to the worm wheel, but back-driving from the wheel is prevented by friction at the contact surface, acting like a built-in mechanical brake.

\paragraph{Self-Locking Principle and Efficiency}

The lead angle $\gamma$ of the worm depends on the pitch diameter $d_1$, the module $m_n$, and the number of thread starts $z_1$:
\begin{equation}
    \tan \gamma = \frac{z_1 \cdot m_n}{d_1} = \frac{p_L}{\pi \cdot d_1}
\end{equation}
Due to the normal tooth pressure angle $\phi_n$, the normal force between the teeth is increased, resulting in an effective friction coefficient $\mu'$:
\begin{equation}
    \mu' = \frac{\mu_s}{\cos \phi_n}
\end{equation}
From this, the effective friction angle $\rho'$ is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    \rho' = \arctan(\mu') = \arctan\left(\frac{\mu_s}{\cos \phi_n}\right)
\end{equation}
For static self-locking, the lead angle must be smaller than the effective friction angle:
\begin{equation}
    \gamma < \rho' \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \tan \gamma < \frac{\mu_s}{\cos \phi_n}
\end{equation}
In practical engineering design, self-locking typically occurs when the lead angle is kept below approximately $5^\circ$ (or around $3^\circ$--$4^\circ$ to maintain a safety margin when vibrations are present during operation).

The transmission efficiency is directly coupled to this self-locking condition. When the worm actively drives the wheel (forward direction), the efficiency is:
\begin{equation}
    \eta = \frac{\tan \gamma}{\tan(\gamma + \rho')}
    \label{eq:worm-forward-eff}
\end{equation}
Conversely, when a load attempts to back-drive the worm from the wheel side (reverse direction), the theoretical efficiency is:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\text{rev}} = \frac{\tan(\gamma - \rho')}{\tan \gamma}
    \label{eq:worm-reverse-eff}
\end{equation}
When the self-locking condition $\gamma \le \rho'$ is satisfied, $\tan(\gamma - \rho') \le 0$, meaning the reverse efficiency is zero or negative. In this state, any external torque applied on the output wheel only increases the normal friction force, preventing the worm from rotating.

\section{Controller}
\label{sec:controller}

To fulfill the requirements for the motorized deployable antenna---namely controlled deployment and retraction speed and position (REQ-03, REQ-04, REQ-08 in Table~\ref{tab:technical_requirements}), holding the structure in position without permanent power draw (REQ-06, REQ-07), and closed-loop control in simulation (SIM-02 in Table~\ref{tab:simulation-requirements})---a cascaded position and speed controller architecture was implemented.

Figure~\ref{fig:cascaded-controller} shows the cascaded PID control structure. In this scheme, the outer position controller compares the target position with the actual position to calculate a velocity setpoint for the inner loop. The inner speed controller then regulates the velocity and generates the torque/current command for the motor. This cascaded setup allows tuning the speed and position loops separately, which simplifies parameter adjustments.
    \begin{figure}[htbp]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/controller/cascaded_position_speed_pid.jpg}
        \caption{Cascaded position and speed control structure with inner and outer loops~\cite{doc:synapticon}.}
        \label{fig:cascaded-controller}
    \end{figure}

\paragraph{Outer Position Control Loop (P Controller)}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}(t) = \left(\text{Pos}_{\text{ref}}(t) - \text{Pos}_{\text{meas}}(t)\right) \cdot \text{KP}_{\text{pos}}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Inner Speed Control Loop (PI Controller)}
\begin{equation}
    \text{Torque}_{\text{cmd}}(t) = \left(\text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}(t) - \text{Vel}_{\text{meas}}(t)\right) \cdot \text{KP}_{\text{vel}} + \int_{0}^{t} \left(\text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}(t) - \text{Vel}_{\text{meas}}(t)\right) \cdot \text{KI}_{\text{vel}} \, dt
\end{equation}

\newpage
\subsection{Realization of a Cascaded Controller}
\label{subsec:realization-of-a-cascaded-controller}

For the discrete implementation, an explicit Euler integration method is used for the inner velocity loop. To account for physical motor torque limits and avoid integrator windup, the integral accumulator is clamped to defined minimum and maximum limits. Listing~\ref{lst:cascaded-pi-euler} shows the pseudo-code implementation of the cascaded controller.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=vscodelight, caption={Discrete realization of the cascaded position (P) and speed (PI) controller using explicit Euler integration.}, label={lst:cascaded-pi-euler}]
// PI Update (Explicit Euler: integral += Ki * error * dt)
function update_pi(error, dt, Kp, Ki, ref_integral, min_val, max_val):
    ref_integral = clamp(ref_integral + Ki * error * dt, min_val, max_val)
    return clamp(Kp * error + ref_integral, min_val, max_val)

// Cascaded Step
function control_step(pos_target, pos_actual, speed_actual, dt):
    // Outer Loop: Position -> Speed setpoint (P Controller)
    speed_target = clamp(Kp_pos * (pos_target - pos_actual), -MAX_SPEED, MAX_SPEED)
    
    // Inner Loop: Speed -> Torque demand (PI Controller)
    torque_out   = update_pi(speed_target - speed_actual, dt, Kp_vel, Ki_vel, spd_int, -MAX_TORQUE, MAX_TORQUE)
    
    return torque_out
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Modeling}
\label{sec:modeling}

The overall system was modeled using Simulink and Simscape. This allowed combining the controller algorithms in Simulink with the physical multibody mechanics of the antenna in Simscape.

\subsection{Modeling of the Controller}
\label{subsec:simulation-of-the-controller}

The Simulink implementation of the inner PI speed controller is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pi-speed-controller}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/controller/PI_Speed_Controller.png}
    \caption{Simulink implementation of the PI speed controller with integral anti-windup clamping.}
    \label{fig:pi-speed-controller}
\end{figure}

In the proportional path, the speed error $e_{\text{vel}} = \text{Vel}_{\text{ref}} - \text{Vel}_{\text{meas}}$ is multiplied directly by the proportional gain $\text{KP}_{\text{vel}}$. In the lower integral path, the error is scaled by $\text{KI}_{\text{vel}}$ and the sample time $\Delta t$, and fed into an accumulator consisting of a sum block and a unit delay $\frac{1}{z}$ to perform discrete Euler integration. A saturation block directly after the delay limits the accumulator state to prevent integrator windup when the motor operates at its torque limits. The proportional and clamped integral signals are then added together to form the motor torque command.

Figure~\ref{fig:p-position-controller} shows the outer position controller loop.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/controller/P_Position_Controller.png}
    \caption{Simulink implementation of the switching P-position controller with directional gain adaptation.}
    \label{fig:p-position-controller}
\end{figure}

The position loop computes the error $e_{\text{pos}} = \text{Pos}_{\text{ref}} - \text{Pos}_{\text{meas}}$ between the target angle and the actual shaft angle. During ground testing under gravity, the actuation dynamics are asymmetric: moving the mechanism upward requires extra torque to lift against gravity, while moving downward is assisted by gravity. To obtain consistent movement in both directions, a MATLAB function block selects between two different position gains based on the sign of the error:

\begin{equation}
    \text{KP}_{\text{pos}} = 
    \begin{cases} 
        \text{KP}_{\text{pos,up}} & \text{if } e_{\text{pos}} \ge 0 \quad (\text{upward movement}), \\
        \text{KP}_{\text{pos,down}} & \text{if } e_{\text{pos}} < 0 \quad (\text{downward movement}).
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The position error is multiplied by the selected gain to generate the velocity setpoint $\text{Vel}_{\text{ref}}$ fed into the inner speed controller.

\subsection{Modeling of the Motor}
\label{subsec:simulation-of-the-motor}

The dynamic model of the DC motor is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:motor-model}, incorporating electrical circuit dynamics and mechanical damping losses. The corresponding motor parameters are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:motor-parameters}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig/controller/motor_model.png}
    \caption{Simulink implementation of the DC motor dynamic model.}
    \label{fig:motor-model}
\end{figure}

The electrical behavior of the armature circuit is described by Kirchhoff's voltage law, accounting for winding resistance, inductance, and the induced back-EMF:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{di(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{L_A} \left( V_{\text{input}}(t) - R_a \cdot i(t) - c_w \cdot \omega(t) \right)
\end{equation}
where $V_{\text{input}}(t)$ is the input terminal voltage, $R_a$ the armature resistance, $L_A$ the armature inductance, $c_w$ the motor constant, and $\omega(t)$ the rotor angular velocity. In the Simulink block diagram, the resulting voltage difference is scaled by $1/L_A$ and integrated to obtain the armature current $i(t)$.

The electromagnetic torque generated by the motor is proportional to current:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{shaft}}(t) = c_w \cdot i(t)
\end{equation}
Subtracting viscous damping losses yields the net mechanical torque delivered to the gearbox:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{motor}}(t) = T_{\text{shaft}}(t) - C_{\text{Damp}} \cdot \omega(t) = c_w \cdot i(t) - C_{\text{Damp}} \cdot \omega(t)
\end{equation}
where $C_{\text{Damp}}$ is the viscous friction coefficient. The rotor inertia itself is accounted for directly on the input side of the Simscape transmission model due to the direct mechanical coupling.

\subsection{Modeling of the Transmission}
\label{subsec:simulation-of-the-transmission}

Figure~\ref{fig:transmission-model} shows the Simscape model of the transmission. It includes the worm gear block, shaft inertia, and viscous damping blocks. In addition, an engagement logic block with a controllable clutch is added to properly simulate self-locking holding torque.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig/controller/transmission_model.png}
    \caption{Simscape implementation of the self-locking transmission model.}
    \label{fig:transmission-model}
\end{figure}

The transmission model consists of several key elements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gear and Inertia:} The Simscape worm gear block defines the kinematic reduction ratio, while rotational inertia blocks model the mass moments of inertia of the input worm and output shaft.
    \item \textbf{Numerical Damping:} Small rotational damping blocks are placed on both shafts to provide necessary numerical damping and prevent solver instability.
    \item \textbf{Clutch and Locking Logic:} A controllable friction clutch block acts as a holding brake when the motor is turned off.
    \item \textbf{Domain Interfaces:} An Ideal Torque Source converts the Simulink torque command into physical network torque, and connection ports interface the transmission output with the multibody satellite model.
\end{itemize}

The clutch engagement logic is implemented in a MATLAB function block shown in Listing~\ref{lst:clutch-control}.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=vscodelight, language=Matlab, morekeywords={persistent, isempty}, caption={MATLAB function script governing clutch engagement logic for self-locking simulation.}, label={lst:clutch-control}]
function y = fcn(input_torque)
persistent y_state;

% Initialize y_state on the very first execution
if isempty(y_state)
    y_state = 0; 
end

if abs(input_torque) <= 10^-14
    y = 1;
    y_state = 1;
    return
elseif abs(input_torque) >= 10^-12
    y = 0;
    y_state = 0;
    return
end

y = y_state;
\end{lstlisting}

The function uses threshold-based hysteresis to switch the clutch state: when the input torque drops below $|T_{\text{in}}| \le 10^{-14}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, the motor is regarded as stopped and the clutch output switches to $y=1$, locking the output shaft in place. When driving torque is applied ($|T_{\text{in}}| \ge 10^{-12}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$), the clutch disengages ($y=0$) to allow motion. Within the small intermediate deadband ($10^{-14}$ to $10^{-12}\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$), the persistent state variable \texttt{y\_state} keeps the previous output value, which avoids numerical chattering and solver issues around zero torque.
